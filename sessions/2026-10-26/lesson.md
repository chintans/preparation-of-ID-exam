## Lesson 1: Remuneration of independent directors (A6)

### What an ID may and may not receive

| Item | Provision | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Stock options | Sec 149(9), Sec 197(7) | An ID gets **no stock options (ESOPs)**. Ever. |
| Sitting fees | Sec 149(9), Sec 197(5); Rule 4, Companies (Appointment and Remuneration of Managerial Personnel) Rules 2014 | Allowed. Max **Rs 1 lakh per board or committee meeting**. An ID's fee must **not be lower** than the fee paid to other directors. |
| Reimbursement | Sec 149(9) | Expenses for attending board and other meetings are allowed. |
| Profit-related commission | Sec 149(9), Sec 197(1) | Allowed, if **members approve**. |
| Pay in a loss / low-profit year | Sec 149(9) proviso and Sec 197(3), inserted by the Companies (Amendment) Act 2020, effective **18 Mar 2021**; Schedule V Section II | Allowed, within Schedule V limits (below). |

### Overall caps (Sec 197(1), net profit computed under Sec 198)
- All directors and the manager together: **11%** of net profit.
- Non-executive directors (NEDs, IDs included) as a group: **1%** if the company has an MD, WTD or manager; **3%** if it has none.
- To pay more than these caps, members must pass a **special resolution** (Sec 197(1) fourth proviso, since the 2017 amendment; Central Government approval is no longer needed).
- **Sitting fees sit outside these caps** (Sec 197(5)).

### Schedule V Section II: no profits or inadequate profits (NEDs and IDs, per year, sitting fees apart)

| Effective capital | Max for an NED / ID |
|---|---|
| Negative or below Rs 5 crore | Rs 12 lakh |
| Rs 5 crore to below Rs 100 crore | Rs 17 lakh |
| Rs 100 crore to below Rs 250 crore | Rs 24 lakh |
| Rs 250 crore and above | Rs 24 lakh + 0.01% of effective capital above Rs 250 crore |

These are roughly one-fifth of the limits for managerial personnel (60 / 84 / 120 lakh). A special resolution lets the company pay up to double.

### Extra rules for listed companies (LODR Reg 17(6))
- **Reg 17(6)(a):** the board recommends all fees and compensation for NEDs (IDs included), and shareholders approve them in a general meeting.
- **Reg 17(6)(b):** that shareholder approval is **not** needed for sitting fees within the Companies Act limits.
- **Reg 17(6)(ca):** if one NED is to get **more than 50%** of the total annual remuneration of all NEDs, shareholders must approve it by **special resolution every year**.

### Worked examples

**Case 1: commission cap.** Kappa Ltd has an MD. Its Sec 198 net profit is Rs 40 crore. All NEDs together can get commission of up to **1% = Rs 40 lakh** without a special resolution. Sitting fees of Rs 75,000 per meeting are on top and do not count toward the Rs 40 lakh.

**Case 2: lean year.** Lambda Ltd made a loss this year. Its effective capital is Rs 60 crore. Before March 2021 its IDs could get only sitting fees. Now each ID may get up to **Rs 17 lakh** for the year (plus sitting fees), if the company's policy and the shareholder resolution allow it.

**Case 3: the ESOP offer.** Mu Ltd wants to keep a star ID by granting 10,000 ESOPs. **It can't.** Sec 197(7) bars it. Commission approved by members is the lawful route.

### Common exam traps
- Rs 1 lakh is the cap **per meeting**, not per year.
- The 1% / 3% NED cap depends on whether there is an **MD, WTD or manager**. With one, it is 1%.
- Sitting fees are **outside** the 11% / 1% / 3% caps.
- "IDs can be paid nothing in a loss year" was true only **before 18 Mar 2021**.

## Lesson 2: Liability of independent directors (A7)

### The protective rule: Sec 149(12)
Notwithstanding anything else in the Act, an ID, and a non-executive director who is not a promoter or KMP, is liable **only** for acts of omission or commission by the company that happened:
1. **with their knowledge, attributable through board processes**, or
2. **with their consent or connivance**, or
3. **where they had not acted diligently**.

Being on the board when something went wrong is **not**, by itself, enough.

### Related provisions
- **Sec 2(60), "officer who is in default":** clause (vi) covers a director who knew of a contravention because they received board proceedings or took part in them without objecting, or with whose consent or connivance it happened. Your **recorded dissent** in the minutes is your evidence.
- **Sec 463:** a court may relieve an officer, wholly or partly, who acted **honestly and reasonably** and ought fairly to be excused.
- **MCA General Circular 01/2020 (2 Mar 2020):** Registrars should not proceed against IDs and non-promoter NEDs without evidence that Sec 149(12) applies. They should check the board minutes and other records first.
- **IBC Sec 66(2) (wrongful trading):** a director can be made to contribute if they knew, or ought to have known, that insolvency could not be avoided and did not take due diligence to minimise losses. Diligence matters here too.

### What "acting diligently" looks like in practice
Read the board papers. Ask questions and get the answers minuted. Record dissent. Follow up on audit qualifications and whistle-blower complaints. Don't approve items tabled without papers. Resign, with your reasons, if concerns are ignored (LODR Reg 30 requires the resignation letter and its detailed reasons to be disclosed).

### Worked examples

**Case 1: dissent recorded.** At Nu Ltd's board meeting, Ms Pillai (ID) objects to a large unsecured loan to a promoter entity, and her dissent is minuted. The loan is later found to breach Sec 185. She is **not liable**: she had no consent or connivance, and she acted diligently.

**Case 2: the silent ID.** Mr Gupta (ID) sat on Xi Ltd's audit committee for two years while the statutory auditor flagged unreconciled bank balances in every report. He never asked a question. When fraud surfaces, Sec 149(12) does **not** protect him. The issue reached him through board processes and he did not act diligently.

### Common exam traps
- Sec 149(12) also protects **non-executive, non-promoter, non-KMP** directors, not only IDs.
- "Knowledge" means knowledge **through board processes** (papers, minutes, meetings), not rumours.
- Lack of diligence is a ground for liability on its own. You can be liable without consent or connivance.
